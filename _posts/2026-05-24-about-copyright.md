## Historical notes

Media piracy, today, is treated as if it were a *moral* crime, but we forget that the right to copy - copyright - is a fairly recent invention and was born, in England, with a fundamental objective: **censorship**.

It was born in the XVI century and, given the enormous volume of books suddenly published after the invention of the printing press, the English government thought to limit the publication of books that went against national security: the **London Company of Stationers** was born.

The Stationers - that is booksellers and printers - were paid for each copy, a sort of reward for controlling the works that were printed and produced: they also had the right to confiscate and burn books printed *illegally*.

A fine privilege that lasted about a century and a half, on the backs of authors and against the right to knowledge. Were they really so different from the Inquisition?

Subsequently, with the rise of more liberal ideas, fearing that independent publishers would snatch their profits, and appealing to parliament, the Stationers managed to obtain that authors had a *property right* over their work, but that this right could be transferred like a contract, and here lies the cunning: authors could certainly not afford to print with those expensive machines so, for publication, they were forced to sign and to transfer the profits themselves to the publishers.

## Today

The situation remained almost unchanged until the arrival of mass storage and the subsequent internet which allowed the costs of copying to be almost zeroed out and thus brought the real bottleneck to the surface: publishers.

In fact those who really profit from the distribution - commerce? - of artistic creations are the publishers themselves: have you ever seen what kind of royalties artists receive?

Why should I pay rights to publishers? Why not pay only the production costs as if it were a simple factory of media supports? The business should be based on the fact that an industry, for example the music industry, sells physical supports, such as CDs, at a price to make a profit from the sale itself - like anyone producing a physical good - and paid the artists themselves for the commercial license.

There should be no rights for industries, both because they have not created anything, and because they only provide supports or infrastructures to enjoy the works. The same could apply to streaming services, such as Spotify: I would pay for the service itself, which would make a profit on its infrastructure - effectively offering this - and paying the artists directly, without going through intermediaries.

So it is correct to pay for a CD, DVD, cinema ticket, streaming infrastructure, because we are effectively paying for a service and the provider rightfully makes their profit, but wanting to tax immaterial goods - like data - under the pretense of *theft*, and appealing to copyright, is truly senseless: what would I be stealing, materially? Only imaginary rights imposed by those who profit from the artists/intellectuals themselves? One might counter: "You are not stealing materially but you are damaging the economic model that supports this"; fine, but the economic model is wrong then, and that is what I am talking about.

In American law there is the case of *fair use*, that is a fair use of a copyrighted work, perhaps for educational purposes: I propose that any consumption of an artistic work for non-commercial purposes should be fair use, because denying a human being the viewing of works, the reading of ideas and even scientific articles - look at **SciHub** and the system of scientific articles of American journals - hinders the very freedom of man, which extends therefore into the freedom to learn, choose and develop one's own faculties, knowledge and values.

> *"Since men can only think, feel, value and act as individuals, it is vital for the survival and prosperity of each to be free to learn, choose, develop their own faculties and act according to their own knowledge and values."*
>
> <small>Murray Rothbard, For a New Liberty</small>

Furthermore it creates a very serious social divide: certainly members of the *upper classes* have no problem paying for iTunes, or Netflix, or whatever service, but a person of modest means, or even one from the third world, living on very few dollars, would be completely cut off from the possibility of elevating their spirit and also their social position. Are we effectively openly admitting that knowledge has a cost and that those who cannot afford it must stay where they are?

> *"Information is power. But like all power, there are those who want to keep it for themselves. The world's entire scientific and cultural heritage, published over centuries in books and journals, is increasingly being digitized and locked up by a handful of private corporations. Want to read the papers featuring the most famous results of the sciences? You'll need to send enormous amounts to publishers like Reed Elsevier."*
>
> <small>Aaron Swartz, Guerilla Open Access Manifesto</small>

All of this should be extended to any intellectual creation, including software development as, for example, the **Free Software Foundation**, has masterfully argued for years. Denying software and the possibility of studying it is a limitation to the development of man himself.

Before the famous **Open Letter to Hobbyists** by Bill Gates, the culture of software, which almost coincided with hacker values, was for the full freedom of exchange of software itself and the desire to improve systems: in fact software was distributed together with hardware, and the profit of companies was the physical support itself. In that letter, Gates instead created the sense of *proprietary software* that we have today, with a license of use - which even obliges us on how we must use it - and with a closed source code - for those who do not understand what this means, just think that the proprietary software you use is not inspectable, not modifiable, and you cannot really understand what it does and does not do; it could perfectly well be tracking you and you might perfectly well not know it: in short it is a closed black box. This also goes against both the freedom of man, the freedom to learn, but also the respect of users.

## Economic model

Let us therefore divide into two uses: personal and commercial. Artists should be paid **only** for commercial purposes, and directly by those who distribute their art/intellectual product, like a simple transaction between private parties. This should neither be monopolized nor centralized as happens today, and it is the industries themselves that should pay the artist directly in order to make a profit from the distribution of their works. A new system should not even be created, it would simply be enough to not prosecute personal uses.

Furthermore the donation model is a good way for ordinary people to finance authors, and the case of **Radiohead** with **In Rainbows** is a nice example, which was produced without a record label and uploaded directly online on their website: whoever wanted could download it for free or make a free donation. Thom Yorke revealed that it was the album from which they earned the most money - with the profits going directly to them - and that about 40% of the people who downloaded it paid.

One might say: this can work with a famous artist, but what about emerging ones? Counter-question: *how much do you think an emerging artist earns with Spotify?* A few euros? The major profits go to intermediaries, if they have one. Otherwise they usually distribute their art for free and monetize with other means.

We should educate society to donate to authors - whether they create music, films, software - because they are working for us, for our development; allowing this alternative economic model would free them from their current employer, who greedily profits on their backs and on our right to evolve.

De-monopolizing the major publishers would also be a way to allow more circulation of art and creativity, blocking this system that imposes on those with more money to bring forward the artist they have chosen and maintain a cultural predominance - after all it still maintains its original nature, that is censorship. Giving less power to publishers could allow a greater diffusion of works that would otherwise have remained obscure to the public due to cultural predominance and allow a more equitable and varied development of society.
